Key exchange in public

Two strangers want a shared secret key, but every message between them can be read by an eavesdropper. That sounds impossible, and Diffie-Hellman does it anyway. HTTPS, SSH, Signal and WireGuard all start this way.

1. The paint version

Mixing paint is easy, and un-mixing it is practically impossible. Alice and Bob agree in public on a starting colour. Each adds a secret colour of their own and sends the result. Then each adds their own secret to what they received.

3. Can Eve just try every exponent?

Eve knows p, g and A. She can try g¹, g², g³, … until one equals A. Let's time her.

For scale, the universe is about 1.4 × 1010 years old. Each extra bit doubles the work. Real keys use 2048-bit primes or elliptic curves. Smarter attacks than brute force exist, and that's why the sizes are so large, but even those stay far beyond reach at these sizes.

4. The catch: who are you talking to?

Tick Mallory in the middle above. If an attacker can change messages, not just read them, she can run one exchange with Alice and another with Bob. Each side ends up sharing a perfectly good key… with Mallory. She decrypts, reads and re-encrypts everything.

Diffie-Hellman alone gives secrecy, not identity. That's why HTTPS pairs it with certificates. The server signs its half of the exchange with a key that a trusted authority has vouched for, so Mallory can't swap in her own.

Toy sizes, built for clarity. Never roll your own crypto for real use.
