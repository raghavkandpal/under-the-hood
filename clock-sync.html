Clock synchronisation

Every computer's clock drifts, a few seconds a week for a cheap crystal. Logs from different machines stop lining up, certificates look expired, and “which write happened first?” gets the wrong answer. Fixing a clock means asking a better one for the time, over a network whose delay you can't see. NTP's trick is to ask, time the round trip, and split the difference.

How it works

Four timestamps. Your computer notes when it sends a request (t1, by its own clock). The server notes when it arrives (t2) and when it replies (t3), by its clock. You note when the reply lands (t4). The round trip spent on the network is δ = (t4 − t1) − (t3 − t2), with the server's processing time removed. Assuming the trip took equally long each way, the server's clock is ahead of yours by θ = ((t2 − t1) + (t3 − t4)) / 2.

The unavoidable error. Nothing can tell how the round trip was split. If the request took 40 ms and the reply 10 ms, the estimate is off by 15 ms, half the difference. That's the fundamental limit: the error is never more than half the round trip, so a nearby server and a fast network give tighter time.

Filtering. Most delay is queueing in routers, which only ever adds time, randomly. Samples with long round trips are the least trustworthy. Plot offset against delay and the points form a wedge whose tip, the shortest round trip, sits closest to the truth. NTP keeps the lowest-delay sample out of the last eight rather than averaging. Averaging can't fix a consistently asymmetric path, though: no client-side trick can see it.

Drift and discipline. A typical crystal is off by 10–100 parts per million, up to about 9 seconds a day. NTP measures the drift rate and gently speeds up or slows down the clock rather than jumping, because time going backwards breaks software. Leap seconds are usually “smeared” across a day for the same reason.

Data centres use PTP with hardware timestamps for sub-microsecond accuracy, and Google's Spanner uses GPS and atomic clocks with an explicit uncertainty interval, waiting it out before committing. When you can't trust clocks at all, use logical time: vector clocks.
