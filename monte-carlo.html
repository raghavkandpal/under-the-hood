Monte Carlo methods

Some questions are too messy to solve with a formula but easy to simulate. Run the random process thousands of times and count what happens. Physicists, financiers, engineers and game AIs all lean on it, and it starts with throwing darts.

1. Estimating π with darts

Estimate as darts accumulate (log scale), with the true π dashed. Each 100× more darts buys about one more correct digit.

2. When will the project finish?

Each task has a best case, a likely case and a worst case (days). Tasks run one after another.

What's going on

Darts. Throw uniformly random darts at a square with a circle inside. The share that lands in the circle approaches the area ratio, π/4, so 4 × (share inside) estimates π. No geometry or calculus needed, just counting.

The catch is speed. The error shrinks like 1/√n. Ten times the accuracy costs a hundred times the samples. But that rate doesn't depend on how many dimensions the problem has, which is why Monte Carlo wins for high-dimensional problems where grids explode.

Planning with uncertainty. Adding up each task's likely duration gives a plan you'll almost always miss, because overruns are bigger than underruns: a task can't take less than zero days but can easily take double. Simulating thousands of possible projects gives a distribution of finish dates, so you can quote "90% confident by day X" instead of a single number that's probably optimistic.

Named after the Monte Carlo casino by Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann, who used it for neutron diffusion in the 1940s. Task durations here follow triangular distributions; 5,000 simulated projects per update.
