CPU scheduling

Your laptop runs hundreds of programs on a handful of cores. The operating system's scheduler keeps choosing who runs next, and different choices make the same work feel fast or sluggish. Edit the jobs and compare four classic strategies on one core.

Presets:

wait = time spent ready but not running · response = time until first run · ★ = lowest average wait

Four strategies

First come, first served is simple and fair in one sense, but a long job at the front makes every short job behind it wait. That's the convoy effect, like being stuck behind a full trolley at a checkout.

Shortest job first runs the quickest job next, which provably minimises average waiting time when all jobs are known. The catch: the OS rarely knows how long a job will take, and a stream of short jobs can starve a long one forever.

Shortest remaining time is SJF that can preempt. A new short job interrupts a long one. Even better averages, more context switches.

Round robin gives each job a small time slice in turn. Nobody waits long to start (good response time, which is what makes interactive programs feel snappy), but long jobs finish later and every switch has overhead. Shrink the slice to see switches climb, or grow it until RR behaves like FCFS.

Real schedulers mix these ideas. Linux's CFS and its successor EEVDF give each task a fair share of CPU time weighted by priority. Many systems use multi-level feedback queues that guess "interactive vs. batch" from past behaviour. Context-switch cost isn't charged in this simulation.
