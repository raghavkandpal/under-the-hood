Neural networks & backprop

A neural network is a big formula with adjustable numbers in it. Training means nudging those numbers to make the error smaller. Backpropagation is how it figures out which way to nudge every one of them at once, using the chain rule from calculus.

1. One neuron, by hand

This neuron computes a = σ(w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b) and is scored by L = (a − y)². Orange boxes are the parameters it can learn. Run the forward pass, then walk the gradient backwards one node at a time.

Green numbers are gradients: how much the loss would change if you wiggled that box. Each node only needs its own local derivative. Multiply it by the gradient flowing in from the right and pass the result left. That's the chain rule, and backprop is just doing it in the right order so nothing is computed twice. A network with a billion parameters does exactly this, over many more boxes.

Press Apply update a few times. Each parameter moves a little against its gradient, and the loss drops. That's one step of gradient descent, and training is millions of these.

2. A real (small) network

Stack neurons in layers and the network can bend its decision boundary into almost any shape. Pick a dataset and watch it learn. Orange and blue are the two classes, and the shading is what the network currently predicts everywhere.

Edges: blue = positive weight, red = negative, thickness = size.

Try it: the spiral with no hidden layer can only draw a straight line, so it gets stuck near 50%. Give it 16 → 16 → 16 and it winds the boundary around the arms. XOR with no hidden layer is the famous case that stalled neural network research in 1969.

Part 1's gradients come from a small autograd engine in lib/autograd.js, written for this site and checked against numerical derivatives. Part 2 trains with a hand-written backprop over plain arrays. It's the same maths, tested to give identical updates, but around 100× faster, much as real frameworks compute whole layers at once instead of one number at a time.
