Retrieval-augmented generation

A language model knows nothing about your company's handbook. Retraining it every time the handbook changes is far too slow, so instead you find the few paragraphs relevant to each question and paste them into the prompt. That's RAG, the idea behind most “chat with your documents” tools, and almost every failure comes from the retrieval step, not the model.

How it works

  
1. Chunk. Documents are split into passages of a few dozen to a few hundred words. Too big and each chunk drags in irrelevant text, using up the model's limited context. Too small and a fact gets cut in half: drag the chunk size down to 8 and “within 30 days” ends up separated from “return”.

  
2. Embed and index. Each chunk becomes a vector, so that similar meanings land close together (see embeddings). Here it's a simple word-count vector, which is easy to inspect.

  
3. Retrieve. Embed the question the same way and take the k nearest chunks by cosine similarity. A question that needs two facts from different places needs k ≥ 2. Try the membership question with k = 1.

  
4. Generate. Paste the chunks into the prompt with an instruction to answer only from them. The model's job is now reading comprehension, which it's very good at. If the right chunk wasn't retrieved, a well-behaved model says it doesn't know, and a less careful one makes something up that sounds right.

  
Why real systems use learned embeddings. The tune-up question shares no words with the handbook, which talks about a “service”, so word matching scores every chunk zero. Neural embeddings place “tune-up” near “service”. Production systems often combine both, plus a re-ranking model, and cite which chunk each claim came from.

  
The handbook is invented for this page. Real pipelines chunk on paragraph or heading boundaries rather than fixed word counts, and store millions of vectors in an approximate nearest-neighbour index.
