Nearest neighbours

The laziest machine-learning algorithm there is: to label something new, find the most similar examples you've already seen and let them vote. No training at all, and it works surprisingly well, right up until your data has too many dimensions.

How it works

Predict by asking the neighbours. Measure the distance from the new point to every stored example, take the k closest, and go with the majority. The shaded background is what the model would predict at each spot. The lines where colours meet are its decision boundary.

k is the bias–variance dial. With k = 1 every training point is classified perfectly, which only means it's memorised. The boundary wraps around every noisy outlier and new points get misclassified. A larger k averages away noise but blurs real detail, and at k = all points it just predicts the majority class everywhere. The held-out accuracy classifies each point using all the others (leave-one-out cross-validation), the honest way to pick k. Try the Noisy dataset.

Weighting lets closer neighbours count more, and makes the boundary less sensitive to k.

The curse. In 2-D the nearest neighbour is far closer than the farthest. In 1,000 dimensions, every point is roughly the same distance from every other, so “nearest” stops meaning much. Real data is often nicer than random noise (images lie near a lower-dimensional surface), which is why kNN on learned embeddings powers recommendation and semantic search. With millions of points, approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (HNSW, IVF) avoid checking every one.

Distances only make sense if features are on comparable scales. Measure height in mm and age in years and height dominates, so features are usually standardised first.
