Linear regression

Given a cloud of points, which straight line fits best? Least squares answers: the one that makes the total area of these squares as small as possible. Every point gets a vote, which is also its weakness.

How it works

Residuals. For any line, each point misses it by some vertical distance, its residual. Least squares picks the line that minimises the sum of the squared residuals: the total area of the squares on the plot. Squaring makes misses above and below count the same, and makes big misses count much more than small ones.

A formula, not a search. The best line always passes through the average point, and its slope is how x and y vary together divided by how much x varies on its own: cov(x, y) / var(x). No iteration is needed, which is why regression was practical long before computers. Nudge the line any way from there and the squares grow.

Try it. Move your own line until its squares are as small as you can make them, then compare with the least-squares line. Then add an outlier.

R². The share of the variation in y that the line explains: 1 means every point is on the line, 0 means the line does no better than always guessing the average. With more noise it falls, even if the underlying relationship is just as real.

Outliers. Because misses are squared, one point far from the rest pulls hard. A single outlier among twenty points can cut the slope by a third or more. Check residuals before trusting a fit, and reach for robust alternatives (least absolute deviations, Huber loss) when bad data is likely. A good fit also says nothing about cause: see Simpson's paradox.

Simplified: one input variable and a straight line. With many inputs the same idea becomes a matrix equation, and it's what a single neuron with no activation computes; see gradient descent for fitting models without a formula.
