Maximum flow

Water from a reservoir to a city through pipes of different sizes, or data through links of different speeds: how much can get through at once? Keep finding a route with room to spare and pushing more along it. When none is left, a set of full pipes shows exactly where the limit is.

How it works

Augmenting paths. Find any route from source to sink where every pipe has spare capacity, push as much as the tightest pipe allows, and repeat. Each push is an augmenting path. Edmonds–Karp always picks the path with the fewest pipes, found by breadth-first search, which guarantees it finishes after at most a few times nodes × edges pushes.

Undo. A path may also run backwards along a pipe that's carrying flow, which cancels some of that flow and reroutes it. Without this, an early bad choice could block a better arrangement forever. The spare capacity forwards, plus the flow that could be undone backwards, is called the residual network, and when it has no path from s to t, the flow is maximal.

Try it. Run to the end and note the red pipes. Then double a pipe that isn't red, then one that is.

The cut. At the end, split the nodes into those still reachable from s in the residual network and the rest. Every pipe crossing from the first group to the second is full. Those pipes form a cut: remove them and nothing can get through. The max-flow min-cut theorem says the largest flow always equals the smallest cut. So the red pipes are the bottleneck, and widening any other pipe changes nothing.

Uses. Beyond pipes and networks, max flow solves matching problems (assign workers to jobs, students to schools: a pipe of capacity 1 for each allowed pair), image segmentation (the cut separates foreground from background), and finding how many links must fail to split a network in two.

Simplified: the six-node network from the CLRS textbook, integer capacities, and one source and sink. Faster algorithms (push-relabel, and recent near-linear-time ones) are used on large graphs.
