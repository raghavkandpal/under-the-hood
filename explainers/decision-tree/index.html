Decision trees

A decision tree classifies by asking a series of yes/no questions, like "is x₁ below 0.4?". Each answer narrows things down until it reaches a verdict. It's the most readable model in machine learning, and it sits underneath random forests and gradient boosting.

How a tree is grown

Pick the best question. At each node, try every possible threshold on every feature, and keep the split that leaves the two sides purest. Purity here is measured by Gini impurity: 0 when a group is all one class, 0.5 when it's a 50/50 mix.

Recurse. Do the same thing inside each half, and stop when a node is pure, too small (min points per leaf) or too deep (max depth). Each leaf predicts its majority class.

Boxes only. Every question looks at one feature, so the boundaries are always horizontal or vertical lines. A diagonal or circular boundary has to be built as a staircase of many small boxes. Look at "Circle in a ring" at depth 8.

Greedy is short-sighted. On XOR, no single first question helps much, because either half is still close to 50/50. So the root split lands wherever noise happens to make it look slightly better (look at the top node, usually nowhere near 0), and the tree spends extra levels recovering. With some samples a shallow XOR tree fails outright. The algorithm never plans ahead.

Overfitting, again. Unlimited depth isolates every training point in its own box: 100% training accuracy, worse on new data. Limiting depth or leaf size is the fix. In practice people average hundreds of slightly different trees (random forests) or add small trees one at a time to correct each other's errors (gradient boosting), and these are some of the strongest models for tabular data.
