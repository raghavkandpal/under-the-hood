Two-phase commit

You transfer money from an account in one database to an account in another. Both changes must happen, or neither. A crash halfway through must not leave money created or destroyed. Two-phase commit is the classic protocol for this, and its weak spot is just as famous.

How it works

Phase 1, prepare. The coordinator asks every participant: "can you commit this?" Each one does all the work needed to be sure it can (writing to its log, taking locks), then votes. A yes vote is a binding promise: from now on that participant must commit if told to, even after a crash and restart.

Phase 2, decide. If every vote is yes, the coordinator writes COMMIT to its own log, which is the moment the transaction becomes real, and tells everyone. Any no, or any silence, means ABORT. A participant that voted no or never voted can simply roll back.

The blocking problem. A participant that voted yes has given up its right to decide alone. If the coordinator then goes silent, the participant can't commit (maybe someone voted no) and can't abort (maybe the coordinator already told others to commit). It must hold its locks and wait, possibly for hours, until the coordinator recovers. That's why 2PC is called a blocking protocol.

What's done about it: run the coordinator itself as a replicated group using Raft or Paxos, so "the coordinator crashed" stops being a single point of failure. Google Spanner and CockroachDB do this. Or avoid distributed transactions altogether, for example with sagas: a chain of local steps, each with a compensating undo.

Shown with one coordinator and three participants, the classic presumed-nothing variant. The safety property, that nobody commits while someone else aborts, holds in every scenario. Only progress is lost.
