Cross-site request forgery

You're logged in to your bank in one tab. In another, you open a page that quietly submits a transfer form to the bank. Your browser helpfully attaches your bank cookie, and the bank sees a request from you.

  • goes wrong
  • as intended

How it works

Ambient credentials. A session cookie is sent with every request to its site, whoever caused the request: the bank's own page, a form on another site, an image tag, a script. The bank's server sees a valid cookie and has no way to tell that you didn't mean it. The attacker never sees your cookie or the response; they don't need to, because the transfer has already happened.

SameSite cookies. A cookie marked SameSite=Lax isn't sent on requests that other sites start, with one exception: following a link (a top-level GET navigation) still sends it, so links from email and search results open you logged in. That stops forged forms and background fetch(). Browsers now treat cookies with no SameSite setting as Lax. Strict doesn't make the exception, which is safer but means every link from outside lands you logged out.

Safe methods. Lax relies on GET requests being harmless, as HTTP says they should be. A bank that transfers money on GET /transfer?to=… is wide open even with Lax, and with a token too, since links carry no token.

Try it. Compare the evil GET link under Lax and under a token, then see what Strict does to the link from your email.

CSRF tokens. The bank puts a random secret in every form it serves and rejects any POST without it. Another site can make your browser send a request, but it can't read the bank's pages, so it can't know the token. Most web frameworks do this for you. Checking the Origin header works on the same principle: the browser, not the page, says where the request came from.

Not XSS. All of this assumes the attacker's code runs on another site. With cross-site scripting their code runs on the bank's own pages, where it can read the token and every defence here is useless.

Simplified: one session cookie, and the token check skips GETs as most frameworks do. Lax also has a two-minute grace period for new cookies on cross-site POSTs in some browsers.
