Negative weights and Bellman–Ford

Dijkstra's shortest-path search is only right when no edge is negative. Bellman–Ford relaxes every edge over and over instead, which is slower and survives negative weights, and a round that still improves something exposes a negative cycle.

How it works

Why Dijkstra breaks. Dijkstra settles the nearest node and never looks at it again, which is safe only if a longer route can never turn out shorter. Take S→A 2, S→B 5 and B→A −4. The version taught in textbooks, which settles each node once, settles A at 2 before it reaches B, so it reports A = 2. The true shortest route is S→B→A at 1. Versions that put a node back in the queue when it improves get this right but can take exponential time in the worst case.

Try it: on Classic, the stats name A = 2 as Dijkstra's answer and Round 1 shows the true A = 1. Then open Chain back to front and step through the rounds: one new node is reached per round.

Relax everything. Bellman–Ford keeps a distance for each node and, each round, goes through every edge u→v and lowers the distance to v if going through u is shorter. After round k every shortest route that uses at most k edges is correct, so V − 1 rounds are enough. Successful relaxations can therefore total at most (V − 1) × E: 6 on Classic (3 nodes, 3 edges), 25 on the 6-node chain (5 edges, 5 successful relaxations).

Edge order. In this model the edges are relaxed in the order they are listed, and that order decides how many rounds do work. The chain N0→N1→…→N5 listed front to back is solved in 1 changing round, because each relaxation feeds the next one in the same pass. Listed back to front, only one new node is reached per round, so 5 rounds change something. Both finish with the same distances and the same 5 successful relaxations. The worst case, V − 1 rounds, is what the bound counts; a lucky order is not a guarantee. A round that changes nothing ends the run early, so the chain stops after round 2 front to back and round 6 back to front.

Negative cycles. If a cycle's weights sum below 0, going around it again always shortens the route, and there is no shortest path. Then a relaxation still succeeds in round V. Stepping back along each node's predecessor V times must land on the cycle, and following the predecessors from there returns it. In Negative loop that is the loop through A, B and C (the tile starts it at C) with weights 1, −3 and 1, a sum of −1, and the run makes 21 successful relaxations against a bound of 20.

Arbitrage. Give each exchange rate the weight −ln(rate). A path's weights then add up to −ln of the product of its rates, so a negative cycle is a loop of trades that ends with more than it started. The page's four currencies have invented rates, with no fees and no real market behind them. USD→EUR 0.90, EUR→GBP 0.85 and GBP→USD 1.32 multiply to 1.0098, a weight of −0.0098, and Bellman–Ford finds that loop. Of the 20 simple cycles through the 4 currencies, 4 pay more than they cost. Lowering the GBP→USD rate by 1%, to 1.3068, drops that loop to about 0.9997 and removes every one of the 4.

Simplified: the Dijkstra here is the textbook settle-once variant, the exchange rates are invented and every trade is free and unlimited, and counts such as 5 changing rounds hold for the edge orders shown. The distance-vector version of this algorithm, run by routers, is in internet routing.
