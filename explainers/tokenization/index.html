Tokenization

Language models don't read letters or words. They read tokens, chunks of text learned from data. Most modern models build their chunks with byte-pair encoding: start from single characters and keep gluing together the pair that appears most often.

    Why this matters

    Fixed vocabulary, any text. Rare or brand-new words never break the model. They just split into smaller known pieces, down to single characters if need be. Type a made-up word above and watch it fall apart into familiar chunks.

    Common things get cheap. Frequent words become a single token, and rare ones cost several. Models are priced and limited by tokens, not words. A typical English token is about 4 characters, and languages under-represented in the training data need more tokens for the same meaning.

    Models don't see letters. If "strawberry" is one or two tokens, the model never directly sees its individual r's. That's part of why language models are oddly bad at spelling and letter-counting.

    Toy version: lowercase letters only and a tiny corpus. Real tokenizers (GPT, Llama, Claude) work on raw bytes so any Unicode works, train on terabytes of text, and learn 50,000–200,000 merges.
