How it works

Scientific notation in binary. A double is (−1)sign × 1.fraction × 2exponent − 1023. The 52 fraction bits give about 15–17 significant decimal digits, and the 11 exponent bits let the point "float" from about 10−308 to 10308.

Why 0.1 is inexact. In base 10, ⅓ never ends (0.333…). In base 2, one tenth never ends: 0.000110011001100… Cut off after 52 bits, 0.1 is stored as slightly more than 0.1, and 0.2 likewise. Their rounding errors add up to a sum that isn't the double closest to 0.3.

Precision is relative. The gap between neighbouring doubles grows with their size. Near 1 it's about 2×10−16. Above 253 (about 9 quadrillion) it's 2, so odd integers can't be stored, which is why big IDs in JSON can silently change in JavaScript.

Special values. All-ones exponent means ∞ or NaN (not-a-number, e.g. 0/0). All-zeros exponent means zero, including a separate −0, or a tiny subnormal number that fills the gap near zero.

What to do about it: compare with a tolerance (Math.abs(a − b) < 1e-9) instead of ===, store money as integer cents or as decimals, and add many small numbers carefully (sort them, or use Kahan summation).

Bit patterns come straight from your browser's own IEEE 754 arithmetic via DataView, nothing simulated. The exact decimal is computed with BigInt.
