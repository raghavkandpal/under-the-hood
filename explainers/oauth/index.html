OAuth: “Sign in with…”

When an app asks to “Sign in with Google” or read your GitHub repos, you never give it your password. OAuth lets the provider hand the app a limited, revocable key instead. Step through the dance, then turn off the safety features and let an attacker in.

How it works

Four parties. You (in a browser), the app that wants access, the authorization server (Google, GitHub) that knows your password, and the API holding your data. The app bounces your browser to the provider. You log in there and approve a scope such as “read your photos”. The provider bounces you back with a one-time code.

Why a code, and not the token directly? The redirect travels through the browser, where URLs leak into history, logs and other apps. The code is single-use and short-lived. The app trades it for the real access token over a direct server-to-server request.

PKCE (“pixie”). Before starting, the app makes a random secret, the verifier, and sends only its hash, the challenge. To redeem the code it must show the verifier. Whoever intercepts the code doesn't have it. Mobile and single-page apps can't keep a client secret, so PKCE is what protects them, and OAuth 2.1 requires it for everyone.

state is a random value the app remembers in your session and expects back on the callback. It proves the login was one you started. Without it, an attacker can plant their own code and log you in to their account (login CSRF), then collect whatever you type.

Access tokens are short-lived. A refresh token gets new ones without asking again, and revoking the app in your account settings kills both. “Sign in with…” adds OpenID Connect, an ID token saying who you are, on top of this same flow. Hashes here are shortened stand-ins for SHA-256.
