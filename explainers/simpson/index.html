Simpson's paradox

Treatment A beats treatment B for small kidney stones. It also beats B for large stones. So A is better overall, right? In the real data, B "wins" overall. No numbers are wrong. The way the patients were grouped is doing the lying.

Edit the counts (successes / patients)

Try making the two arms see the same mix of easy and hard cases (equal patient counts in each group). The paradox disappears.

What's going on

Overall rates are weighted averages. A's overall success mixes its small-stone rate and its large-stone rate, weighted by how many of its patients had each. Doctors gave A mostly to large stones, the hard cases, so A's average is pulled down by difficulty, not by being a worse treatment. B got mostly easy cases and looks good for the same reason.

The hidden variable is a confounder. Stone size affects both which treatment a patient got and how well they did. Whenever a third factor drives both, the pooled comparison can point the wrong way.

Which answer is right? It depends on the causal story, not on the arithmetic. Here severity influenced treatment choice, so the within-group comparison is the fair one, and A is better. That's why medical trials randomise: random assignment breaks the link between the confounder and the treatment, so the overall comparison becomes trustworthy.
