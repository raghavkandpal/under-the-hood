Replication lag

You change your display name, the page reloads, and the old name is back. Refresh again and it's the new one; once more and it's old again. Nothing is broken: your reads are landing on copies of the database that haven't caught up yet.

How it works

Leader and followers. Most databases scale reads by copying every write from one leader to several followers, and serving reads from the followers. Copying is usually asynchronous: the leader confirms your write as soon as it has it, and the followers apply it a little later. Normally that's milliseconds, but under load, after a network hiccup or during a big migration it can be seconds or minutes.

Reading your own writes. If your next read goes to a follower that hasn't applied your write, your change seems to vanish. The usual fix is to route a user's reads to the leader for a while after they write, or to remember the position of their last write and only read from a follower that has reached it. The window has to be longer than the lag, or the problem comes back.

Try it. Choose “Leader after writing”, then shrink the leader window below the follower's lag.

Monotonic reads. Reading from a random follower each time can be worse than stale: one read sees the new name from a fast follower, the next sees the old name from a slow one, and time appears to run backwards. Pinning each user to one follower (for example by hashing their user id) stops that: you may be behind, but never further behind than last time.

The trade-off. These are session guarantees, weaker than the strong consistency of synchronous replication or a quorum read, and much cheaper. Sending reads to the leader costs leader capacity; pinning users makes load uneven and fails over when that follower dies. Many apps mix them: pin by default, use the leader just after a write.

Simplified: one user, three writes and eight reads after each, two followers whose lag varies by up to ±50%, and no failovers.
