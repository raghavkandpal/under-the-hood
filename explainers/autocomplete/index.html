Tries & autocomplete

Type two letters into a search box and suggestions appear before you finish the third. Checking every word in the dictionary each keystroke would be far too slow. Instead the words are stored in a tree where each step down is one more letter, so “everything starting with pr” is just one branch.

Click a suggestion to “pick” it: its popularity goes up and the ranking adapts. Press Enter to add a new word.

How it works

A tree of letters. The root is the empty string. Each edge adds one letter, so every node is a prefix, and words that share a beginning share a path. Nodes where a word ends are marked (filled circles). Looking up a prefix of length m takes m steps, however many words are stored.

Ranking. Below the prefix there may be thousands of words. Each node also remembers the best popularity score anywhere beneath it, so the search can explore the most promising branches first and stop once it has the top 5, skipping the rest entirely. Compare “nodes visited” with “words a scan would check”.

Learning. Picking a suggestion bumps its score, which is how your phone keyboard learns your words. Real search boxes blend global popularity, your own history and recency.

Saving space. Long chains with no branching (like u-n-c-t-i-o-n after “f”) waste a node per letter. A radix tree merges them into one edge labelled with the whole chunk. Routers use the same idea to match IP address prefixes, and so do many web frameworks to match URL routes.

Typo tolerance (“fucntion”) needs more: walking the trie while allowing a few edits, or a separate fuzzy index.
