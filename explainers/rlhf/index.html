Learning from human feedback

A language model trained on text can write a reply, but not tell which of two replies a person would rather read. Human feedback fixes that in two steps: learn a reward from people's choices between replies, then nudge the model towards high reward without letting it wander far from where it started. Here you can watch both steps, and the way the second one goes wrong when the reward is a little off.

How it works

Learning a reward from choices. Nobody can write down a score for "a good answer", but people can say which of two answers they prefer. The Bradley–Terry model turns that into numbers: give each reply a reward r, and say the chance that A is preferred to B is σ(rA − rB). Equal rewards give 0.5, and a gap of ln 3 ≈ 1.1 gives 0.75. Training adjusts the reward until the pairs people chose come out likely, which is logistic regression on the difference between two replies' features. Here a reply has two features, a cue that it sounds helpful and its length. The simulated raters follow true quality but also like a long answer.

How much feedback. Over 8 seeds, a reward model fitted to 2 pairs gets fewer than 75% of fresh pairs right on average. At 50 pairs it is right on about 85%, and the raters themselves, who are noisy, only agree with their own taste about 86% of the time, so there is little left to gain.

Tuning the model against it. The model is nudged, usually with reinforcement learning, to raise the reward. Left alone it would pour all its probability onto whichever reply scores highest, so the objective subtracts β times the KL divergence from the starting model, πref. For a choice among a few replies this has an exact answer, π*(y) ∝ πref(y)·exp(r(y)/β). A huge β leaves the model where it was, and a tiny one puts almost everything on the top-scoring reply. The same formula is what lets Direct Preference Optimization skip the separate reward model. Real models sample from a huge space of text, where this is approached by training rather than computed, but sampling from the tuned model works the same way.

Reward hacking. The reward model learned the raters’ liking for length, so it scores the longest, emptiest reply highest, in all 8 seeds. As β falls the score the model is chasing keeps rising, but the true quality rises and then falls. In this run it reaches 0.41 around β = 2, against 0.34 for the starting model, and drops to 0.11 at β = 0.05 where 98% of the probability sits on the rambling reply. Across the 8 seeds the peak is always at a middle β, between about 2 and 5, though the gain over the starting model is modest: 0.36 to 0.41 against 0.34. The penalty is what holds the model back from the flaw.

Try it. Set Reward A to 1.1 and Reward B to 0. Then drag β from the far right to the far left and watch the true score.

A toy: one prompt, six written replies, a two-feature reward model and an exact optimum, where real systems fit a neural reward model to many prompts and optimise the policy with sampling. The bias towards length is built into the simulated raters.
