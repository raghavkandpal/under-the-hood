Bayes' theorem

A test is "90% accurate" and comes back positive. How likely is it that you really have what it tests for? Most people say about 90%. The real answer can be under 10%, and the reason is the most useful idea in probability.

Why the intuition fails

Count, don't calculate. Out of 1,000 people with a 1% base rate, 10 really have it and 990 don't. The test catches 9 of the 10. But it also falsely flags 9% of the 990, about 89 people. So of 98 positives, only 9 are real. A positive result moved you from 1% to about 9%. That's a big jump, but far from 90%.

The base rate is the hidden half of the evidence. Bayes' theorem says P(real | positive) = P(positive | real) × P(real) / P(positive). The test's accuracy is only the first term. When the thing is rare, the many false alarms from the huge "not real" group swamp the few true hits.

Evidence accumulates. Press "test the positives again". Today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior. A second independent positive pushes 9% to about 50%, and a third to around 90%. That's why screening is usually followed by a different, more specific test.

It's everywhere: spam filters (where spam is common, so flags are mostly right), fraud alerts and airport scanners (where the thing is so rare that almost every alarm is false), and machine-learning classifiers judged by "accuracy" on imbalanced data.

An educational illustration, not medical guidance. Sensitivity is P(positive | real) and the false-alarm rate is P(positive | not real), i.e. 1 − specificity. The test repeats assume each test is independent, which real repeat tests often aren't.
