LoRA fine-tuning

Fine-tuning a large language model normally means updating billions of weights and storing a full copy for every task. LoRA bets that the change a task needs is simple, and learns it as the product of two thin matrices instead.

  • negative

How it works

The update, not the model. Fine-tuning changes each weight matrix W to W + ΔW. Full fine-tuning learns every entry of ΔW: for one 4,096 × 4,096 attention matrix that's 16.8 million numbers, and a model has hundreds of such matrices. Storing a full copy per customer or per task quickly becomes the expensive part.

Low rank. LoRA freezes W and writes the update as ΔW = B·A, where B is d × r and A is r × d, with a small rank r like 8. That's 2 × 4,096 × 8 = 65,536 numbers instead of 16.8 million, 0.4% of the original. Only A and B are trained, so the optimiser's memory shrinks by the same factor, and an adapter for a new task is a few megabytes you can swap in at serving time.

Why it works. A rank-r matrix can only push in r independent directions. The LoRA paper found that fine-tuning updates really are dominated by a few strong directions, so a small rank captures most of them. The bar chart shows the strength of each direction in this update (its singular values): the first few carry most of it, and the tail is close to noise.

Try it. Slide the rank from 1 up to 4, where the strong directions end, then on to 32, and watch where the missed part stops shrinking quickly.

Merging. After training, B·A can be added into W once, so the fine-tuned model runs exactly as fast as the original. QLoRA combines this with a 4-bit quantized frozen model, which lets a single consumer GPU fine-tune models that would otherwise need a server.

Simplified: a 32 × 32 made-up update, and we compute the best rank-r fit directly (truncated SVD) instead of learning A and B by gradient descent, which converges to a similar answer.
