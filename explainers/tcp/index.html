TCP reliability

The internet drops, delays and reorders packets, and doesn't apologise. TCP builds a dependable, in-order byte stream on top of that. Here's how. Click any packet to drop it and watch the connection recover.

The four ideas

1. Handshake. SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. Both sides confirm the other can hear them and agree on starting sequence numbers before sending any data. It costs one round trip before anything useful happens.

2. Sequence numbers and cumulative ACKs. Every segment is numbered. The receiver keeps replying "the next one I need is n". If segment 3 goes missing, every later arrival triggers another "I still need 3".

3. The sliding window. Waiting for each ACK before sending the next segment wastes a whole round trip per segment. Set the window to 1 to feel it. Instead the sender keeps up to window segments in flight. A bigger window means more throughput, until the network can't take it. That limit is what congestion control tunes, and it's left out here.

4. Two ways to notice loss. A retransmission timer fires if the oldest unacknowledged segment waits too long. It's reliable but slow. Fast retransmit treats three duplicate ACKs as proof of a hole and resends at once. Turn it off and drop one segment to see the difference.

Try it: set the window to 6 and click DATA 2 to drop it. The receiver buffers 3–6 while it waits. Three duplicate ACK 2s come back, the sender fills the hole early, and one ACK then jumps straight to 7.

Simplified: segments are counted, not bytes; the RTO is fixed (real TCP measures RTT and backs off exponentially); there's no congestion window, SACK, or connection teardown.
