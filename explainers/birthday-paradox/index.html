The birthday paradox

In a room of 23 people, it's slightly more likely than not that two share a birthday. Most people guess you'd need well over a hundred. The same surprise decides how long your ids can be before two of them collide.

How it works

Count pairs, not people. The intuition goes wrong by asking whether someone shares your birthday. The question is whether any two people do, and 23 people make 253 pairs, each with a 1-in-365 chance of matching. With that many chances, a match becomes likely.

The exact answer. It's easier to compute the chance that all birthdays differ: the second person avoids the first (364/365), the third avoids both (363/365), and so on. Multiply those together and subtract from one. At 23 people that's 50.7%; at 70 it's 99.9%.

Try it. Make a few rooms of 23 and count how many have a shared birthday, then try 40.

The square-root rule. With N equally likely values, collisions become likely after about 1.18 √N items, not N/2. That's why hash sizes come in powers that seem far too big: a 32-bit hash or id collides with even odds after only about 77,000 items, and a 64-bit one after about 5 billion. Random UUIDs have 122 random bits so that collisions stay negligible even across trillions.

In security. Finding any two inputs with the same hash (a collision) takes about the square root of the work of matching one given hash. So a 128-bit hash gives only 64 bits of collision resistance, which is why SHA-256 is used where collisions matter, and why MD5 and SHA-1 were broken by collision attacks first.

Simplified: birthdays are equally likely and there's no 29 February. Real birthdays cluster a little, which makes a match slightly more likely still.
