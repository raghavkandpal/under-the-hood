Write-ahead logging

Move £30 from account A to account B, and the power cuts out halfway. Afterwards the money must be in exactly one place: either the whole transfer happened or none of it did. Every serious database, and most file systems, get there with one simple rule: write down what you're about to do before you do it.

How it works

Why not just write the pages? A transfer touches two places on disk, and disks write one at a time. Crash between the two writes and £30 has vanished. There's no way to update two places atomically, so writing in place can't be safe on its own. Untick the log and try “No log, crash mid-commit”.

The log. Before changing anything, append a record to a sequential log file: “T1 changes A from 100 to 70”. Committing means appending “T1 commits” and forcing the log to disk with fsync. That one append is the moment of truth. The data pages themselves can be written whenever is convenient, minutes later and in big batches, because the log can rebuild them.

The rule: a changed page may reach disk only after the log records describing it. That's why it's called write-ahead. Writing page A early is allowed, and databases do it to free memory, but the engine flushes the log first, so it can always undo.

Recovery. After a crash: redo every change in the log, restoring work that was committed but whose pages never got written, then undo every change from transactions that never committed. That's the essence of ARIES, the algorithm inside most relational databases. Recovery is also safe to run twice, which matters because the power can fail during recovery too.

Bonus: appending to one file is far faster than scattered page writes, so the log makes databases faster as well as safe. The same log, shipped over the network, is how replicas stay in sync (Postgres streaming replication, MySQL binlog), and log-structured storage like LSM trees takes the idea all the way.
