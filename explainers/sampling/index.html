How LLMs choose the next word

A language model doesn't output text. It outputs a probability for every possible next token. Something then has to pick one, and how it picks (temperature, top-k, top-p) decides whether you get boring, creative or nonsense text.

    The three knobs

    Temperature rescales confidence. Below 1 the favourite gets more of the probability. At 0 the model always takes the top word (greedy decoding), which is repeatable but tends to loop and repeat itself. Above 1 the distribution flattens and long-shot words get real chances. Push it to 2 and the sentences fall apart.

    Top-k keeps only the k most likely words and discards the rest. It's a hard cap, so the same k can be too strict when many words are reasonable and too loose when only one is.

    Top-p (nucleus sampling) keeps the smallest set of words whose probabilities add up to p. When the model is sure, that set is tiny. When it's unsure, it widens. Because it adapts, it's the most common default, often around p = 0.9 with a temperature a little under 1.

    Try it: press greedy and re-roll the five completions. All five are identical. Then try wild and watch the grammar dissolve. Somewhere in between is where chatbots usually run.
    The “model” here, and its training text

    A trigram model: it predicts the next word from the previous two, counting what followed them in the training text below and blending in shorter contexts. Real LLMs use transformers with thousands of tokens of context, but the decoding step at the end is exactly this.
