Vector search

Semantic search and RAG store every document as a vector and look for the ones nearest a question. Checking all of them is exact but slow at a billion. An index that groups nearby vectors looks in a few groups instead, and is almost always right.

How it works

Vectors. An embedding model turns text or images into points in a space with hundreds of dimensions, where similar meanings land close together. Finding relevant documents becomes finding the points nearest the query's vector: nearest neighbours. Here the space has two dimensions so it can be drawn, but the method is the same.

Exact search is a full scan. Comparing the query with every stored vector always gives the right answer, and costs one distance per vector. That's fine for a few thousand and far too slow for a billion vectors queried a thousand times a second.

An inverted-file index. Ahead of time, group the vectors with k-means into clusters and keep a list of the members of each. At query time, find the few cluster centres nearest the query, and scan only their lists. With 32 clusters, probing one scans about 4% of the data. The catch: a true neighbour sitting just over a cluster border isn't in the list you scanned. Probing more clusters trades speed back for recall, the share of the true nearest neighbours you find.

Try it. Click near the border between two clusters with one probe, then raise the probes until nothing is missed.

In practice. Vector databases (pgvector, FAISS, and the hosted ones) offer this IVF index alongside graph-based ones like HNSW, which hop between neighbouring points instead. Both are tuned the same way: a recall target, say 95%, against latency. They're also why a RAG system can occasionally miss a document that an exact search would have found.

Simplified: 2,000 points in two dimensions and plain Euclidean distance. Real indexes also compress the vectors (product quantization) so millions fit in memory.
