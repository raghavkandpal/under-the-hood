What to take away

The averages go bell-shaped. Whatever the population looks like (flat, lopsided, two humps), the means of samples pile up into a normal curve once n is moderately large. That's the central limit theorem, and it's why the normal distribution shows up everywhere in statistics.

The spread shrinks with √n. The standard error is σ/√n. Quadrupling the sample halves the uncertainty, and that diminishing return is why polls stop at around 1,000 people. Notice that the population size never appears in the formula.

"95% confident" is a claim about the method, not about one interval. About 1 in 20 intervals misses (red), and you can't tell which. Any one interval either contains the truth or doesn't.

Try it: pick Rare event with n = 5. The sample means look nothing like a bell, and interval coverage drops well below 95%. The CLT is a statement about large n, and skewed or rare-event data needs a bigger n before it kicks in. Push n to 100 and watch it recover.

Intervals use the t distribution (mean ± t0.975, n−1 · s/√n). With n = 1 there's no spread to estimate, so no interval is drawn.
