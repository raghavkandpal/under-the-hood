Error-correcting codes

Cosmic rays flip bits in RAM. Scratches wipe out bits on a CD. Radio noise garbles bits from a Mars rover. Systems survive by adding a few cleverly chosen extra bits, so the receiver can not only detect a mistake but work out which bit is wrong and fix it.

How it works

Overlapping parity checks. Each parity bit makes the count of 1s even across its own group of positions. Bit 1 checks positions 1, 3, 5, 7. Bit 2 checks 2, 3, 6, 7. Bit 4 checks 4, 5, 6, 7. Every position belongs to a different combination of groups.

The syndrome points at the culprit. When a bit flips, exactly the groups containing it fail. Read the failing checks as a binary number (check 4, check 2, check 1) and you get the position of the bad bit. Flip it back and the data is restored. Flip one bit above and watch the failed checks spell out its position.

Limits. Two flips in the same 7-bit block produce a syndrome pointing at the wrong bit, and the "fix" makes things worse. Try it. Real systems add one more overall parity bit to detect double errors (SECDED, used in ECC memory), or use much stronger codes: Reed–Solomon in QR codes, CDs and deep-space links, and LDPC in Wi-Fi, 5G and SSDs.

The trade-off is redundancy against reliability. Hamming(7,4) spends 3 extra bits per 4. Shannon proved in 1948 that, below a channel's capacity, codes exist that make errors as rare as you like, and modern codes come within a whisker of that limit.
