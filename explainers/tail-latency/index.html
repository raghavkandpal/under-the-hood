Tail latency and fan-out

A server that is fast 99% of the time sounds fast. A request that needs a hundred of them is almost always waiting on one of the slow ones.

How it works

One server. In this model, a leaf server answers in 10 ms 99% of the time and in 1,000 ms the other 1%. Its mean is 0.99 × 10 + 0.01 × 1,000 = 19.9 ms, nearly double what 99% of calls take. The mean describes almost no real call, which is why the SRE book measures percentiles instead.

Fan-out. A request that fans out to n leaves waits for the slowest, so it is fast only if all n are: it is slow with probability 1 − 0.99n. That is 1% at n = 1, 9.6% at n = 10 and 63.4% at n = 100. At 100 leaves the median request takes 1,000 ms, because more than half of requests hit a slow leaf. A tail that rare per server becomes the usual case.

Hedging. If a leaf hasn't answered after 20 ms, send the same call to another replica and take whichever comes back first. A leaf is now slow only if both copies are, which has probability 0.01 × 0.01. At n = 100 a request is slow with probability 1 − 0.9999100, about 1%, and the median falls to 30 ms: a slow first copy costs the 20 ms wait plus the 10 ms replica. Only the slow first copies get a second call, so it costs 1% extra calls.

Two copies always. Sending every call twice from the start gives the same slow rate and a faster middle, a 10 ms median at n = 100 against 30 ms, but costs 100% extra calls. Waiting 20 ms gives the same slow rate for a hundredth of the extra work, which is the idea in The Tail at Scale.

Try it. With No hedge, drag Leaves from 1 to 100: the median jumps from 10 to 1,000 ms. Then try Hedge. The seeded tile is a sample, so it strays from the formula.

Every leaf is independent, with latencies of exactly 10 or 1,000 ms. Real slowness is correlated and hedges add load; see retries for what extra load does.
