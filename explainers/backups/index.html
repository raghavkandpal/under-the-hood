Backups and point-in-time recovery

A nightly backup is a day old by evening. Keep the write-ahead log as well and you can rebuild the database as it was at any minute after the backup.

  • recovered
  • lost

How it works

The backup. Every night at 00:00 a base backup copies the database. Alice has 100 and Bob 50 then. If the disk fails at 17:30 and you restore only that backup, you get the 00:00 state and lose all 11 commits made since.

The archive. The database also writes every change to its log, the same log that makes crash recovery work. Here a finished 15-minute segment of that log is copied to another disk. Restore the backup, then replay the archive over it, and you reach the last segment that was shipped. With a failure at 17:30 that is 17:30 itself and nothing is lost. At 17:29 the last shipped segment ends at 17:15, so the 17:25 commit is gone.

How much is lost. A failure at minute t loses the commits made since the last segment boundary, and those are less than 15 minutes of work. It is never a whole day. Shorter segments shrink the window further; they cost more files.

Point in time. At 17:15 someone runs DROP TABLE audit. Replaying the archive to the end replays the drop too, and the table stays gone. Stop the replay at 17:14 and the table is back with its 9 rows, Alice at 35 and Bob at 115. The stop point can be any minute after the base backup ended. 23:00 the day before is refused, because the backup already contains what happened after that.

Try it. On Point in time, drag Stop at between 17:14 and 17:15 and watch the audit table disappear. Then drag it to the day before.

Why the copy may be inconsistent. A backup copies the files while the database keeps running. On the Copy tab, a transfer of 30 from Alice (100) to Bob (50) runs while the copy reads the two rows in order. Of the 6 ways the four steps can interleave, 4 give a total of 150, one gives 180 and one gives 120. Replay fixes it: each log record holds the row's new value, so replaying from the start of the backup overwrites whatever the copy caught, and every one of the 6 totals 150.

One database, a 15-minute segment size and a log of 12 events made up for this page. The log records here hold row values; real WAL records are more involved, and replay depends on full-page images after a checkpoint.
