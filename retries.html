Retry storms

A service hiccups for a few seconds. Every client politely retries its failed requests, and the service, now facing several times its normal traffic, can't come back up. The outage ends, but the failure doesn't. It's one of the most common ways large systems go down, and it's caused by the very code meant to make them reliable.

How it works

Overload is worse than full. Up to its capacity a service handles everything. Beyond it, requests queue, time out, and the work spent on them is wasted, so the number actually served falls as load rises (here, to zero at twice capacity). That cliff is what makes the next part dangerous.

Retries multiply load. A client that retries up to 3 times can send 4× the traffic when everything fails. Set a 3-tick blip with “Retry at once” and watch: 70 requests become 280, the service sits past its cliff, everything keeps failing, so everything keeps retrying. It never recovers, even though nothing is broken any more. This is a metastable failure: the system is stuck in a bad state that sustains itself.

Exponential backoff with jitter waits 2, 4, 8… ticks, randomised so clients don't retry in lockstep. It smooths a short blip almost perfectly. But it only delays the extra traffic and doesn't reduce it, so after a long outage the backlog still swamps the service. Try 20 ticks.

Retry budgets cap retries at a fraction of normal traffic (10% here), so the worst case is 1.1×, not 4×. Circuit breakers notice when most calls fail, stop sending traffic altogether (“open”) and fail fast. Callers get an error or a fallback at once instead of waiting. After a cooldown a few probe requests test the water (“half-open”), and if they succeed traffic resumes. Both deliberately give up on some requests so the system as a whole survives.

Also standard: retry only at one layer (when every layer of a 5-deep call stack retries 3 times, the bottom sees 4⁵ = 1,024×), use timeouts, make retried operations idempotent, and shed load at the front door. Related: rate limiting and queues.
