The Fourier transform

Any signal (a sound, a heartbeat, a stock chart) can be built by adding up simple sine waves. The Fourier transform runs that in reverse: hand it a messy signal and it tells you which waves, and how much of each, are inside. That's how equalisers, MP3s, noise cancelling and Wi-Fi all work.

2. Building a square wave from sines

A square wave is the odd harmonics added together, sin(x) + sin(3x)/3 + sin(5x)/5 + … More terms make sharper corners, but a small overshoot at each jump never goes away (the Gibbs phenomenon).

What's going on

Correlate with every frequency. For each frequency, the transform asks: "how much does this signal look like a sine wave at that speed?" It multiplies the signal by the wave and adds up the result. A matching wave gives a big total, and anything else averages out to nearly zero. The answers, one per frequency, are the spectrum.

Noise spreads out, signals stand out. Random noise contains a little of every frequency, so in the spectrum it's a low, flat carpet. Your three tones remain tall spikes. That's why filtering in the frequency domain works so well: zero out what you don't want and transform back. Turn the noise up, then apply a low-pass filter just above your highest tone.

The FFT (Cooley–Tukey, 1965) computes all of this in n log n steps instead of n², by splitting the signal into even and odd samples recursively. For a million samples that's about 50,000 times faster. Along with sorting, it's among the most important algorithms ever devised.

Where it's used: MP3 and AAC throw away frequencies you can't hear, JPEG does a 2-D cousin (the DCT) on image blocks, Wi-Fi and 4G pack data onto many frequencies at once (OFDM), and audio equalisers boost or cut frequency bands.
